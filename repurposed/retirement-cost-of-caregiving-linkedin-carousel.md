## Feed Caption (post copy, ~1,100 chars)

Most families measure what caregiving costs the way they measure a vacation: by adding up receipts.

Prescriptions. Gas. A grab bar in the shower. A few days of unpaid leave.

The receipts are the small number.

The large one is the income a caregiver never earns and the retirement contributions they never make — often during their highest-earning years. And that cost doesn't stop when the caregiving does. It compounds. Or rather, it fails to.

AARP puts average out-of-pocket caregiving spending at $7,242 a year, about 26% of a caregiver's income — and only about 17% of that is medical. The bigger hit shows up decades later, in a smaller 401(k) balance and a lower Social Security benefit.

The most useful family conversation happens before a crisis: who provides care, who pays, and how the primary caregiver keeps earning and saving.

## Slide 2 — The receipts are the small number

**Body copy:**
AARP: family caregivers spend about **$7,242 a year** out of pocket — roughly **26% of their income**.

Only ~17% report medical costs. Half report general household expenses. 30% help with a parent's rent or mortgage. 21% pay for home modifications.

**Visual:** Single big stat ($7,242 / yr) with a four-item breakdown list beneath.

## Slide 3 — The number nobody counts

**Body copy:**
The larger cost is invisible: **reduced hours, unpaid leave, and paused retirement contributions** during peak earning years.

It doesn't end when the caregiving ends. It keeps growing — because the money that was never invested never compounds.

**Visual:** Two diverging lines — "what you saw" (flat, small) vs "what it cost" (rising).

## Slide 4 — A number with a shape

**Body copy:**
The most-cited estimate: an average lifetime loss of **$303,880** for a caregiver aged 50+ who leaves work — about $115,900 in wages, $137,980 in Social Security, and ~$50,000 in pension value.

Women averaged $324,044. Men, $283,716.

**Caveat (small type):** MetLife Study of Caregiving Costs to Working Caregivers, 2011, with the National Alliance for Caregiving. Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a current price tag.

## Slide 5 — Lost wages were the smallest piece

**Body copy:**
In that estimate, forgone wages were the **smallest** of the three components.

Social Security and pension effects — the parts nobody sees until decades later — were **more than half** the total.

## Slide 6 — The 401(k) math

**Body copy:**
Skip a **$500/month** contribution for **three years** = $18,000 not saved.

That's the number most people have in mind. It's the wrong one.

At a 7% average return, 20 years later that gap is closer to **$77,000** not held in retirement. Add a forgone 3% employer match and it's more.

**Visual:** $18,000 → $77,000 arrow, with 10-yr ($39k) / 15-yr ($55k) / 25-yr ($108k) reference points.
**Source line:** Illustrative; 7% is an assumption, not a promise.

## Slide 7 — Why a career break keeps costing

**Body copy:**
Returning to work is rarely picking up where you left off.

Salary growth restarts from the **old** number. The employer match for those years is gone for good. And because saving is a percentage of income, a permanent step down in pay is a permanent step down in savings rate — long after caregiving ends.

**Visual:** Staircase graphic stepping down, then resuming its climb from a lower step.

---

## Slide 8 — The Social Security connection

**Body copy:**
Your benefit is calculated from your **35 highest-earning years**, indexed for wage growth.

- 35+ strong years already? A few reduced years may drop out — small effect.
- **Fewer than 35?** Every low or zero caregiving year is averaged in directly.
- Caregiving in your peak-earning decade? Those are the most expensive years to lose.

**Source line:** Social Security Administration; Congressional Research Service R46658

---

## Slide 9 — There's no credit to plan around

**Body copy:**
As of 2026, there is **no general federal caregiver tax credit**.

The Credit for Caring Act (up to $5,000 for working caregivers) has been reintroduced in Congress repeatedly. It has not become law. Neither has the Social Security Caregiver Credit Act.

Plan for the rules that exist, not the ones that might.

## Slide 10 — CTA

**Headline:** A family doesn't need an equal division of caregiving. It needs a deliberate one.
**Body copy:**
Before a crisis, put it on the table: what care is needed now and in two years, who can give hours, who can give money, and what it would cost to buy enough help to keep the primary caregiver employed.

Replacing a sibling's $500/month contribution usually costs far less than the retirement gap it prevents.
