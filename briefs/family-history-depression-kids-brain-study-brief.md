## TL;DR Draft
> **TL;DR:** A 2026 Binghamton University study of 242 mother-child pairs found that children whose mothers have a history of major depressive disorder show a distinct attention pattern to sad faces as their own depressive symptoms increase — a pattern not seen in children without that family history. Separately, research shows a parent or sibling with major depression raises a person's own depression risk 2-3x (about 20-30% vs. 10% baseline), and that risk compounds across generations. Family mental-health history is a trackable risk factor, not a certainty — this piece explains what to record and watch for.

### H2: What the New Study Found
- **Answer-first**: Children of mothers with a history of major depressive disorder showed a different attention pattern to sad faces as their own symptoms grew, compared to children without that family history.
- Cover: study design (242 mother-child pairs, assessed every 6 months over 2 years, eye-tracking with paired neutral/emotional faces), what "attention bias" means in plain terms
- **Image**: Photo of parent and young child in warm, everyday interaction (not clinical/sterile)
- **Key stat**: 242 children and mothers, 2-year longitudinal study (Binghamton University / Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science, June 2026)

### H2: Why This Matters Even Though It's Not a Diagnostic Test
- **Answer-first**: The eye-tracking pattern follows depressive symptoms rather than predicting them — it's not a screening tool, but it strengthens the case that family history shapes how children's brains process emotion.
- Cover: distinguish "correlate of current symptoms" from "future predictor" (per Binghamton researchers), why this still matters for awareness

### H2: How Much Does Family History Actually Raise the Risk?
- **Answer-first**: A parent or sibling with major depression roughly doubles to triples a person's own depression risk.
- Cover: 2-3x risk / ~20-30% vs. 10% baseline (Stanford Medicine); 4-5x for early-onset recurrent depression; multi-generational compounding
- **Chart**: Bar chart — depression risk by family history level (no family history baseline ~10-11%, one affected parent/sibling ~20-30%, parent + grandparent ~54% for anxiety disorder specifically — label axes precisely per source)
- **Key stat**: 54% of children with both a depressed parent and grandparent had an anxiety disorder, vs. 11% of children with no family history (Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health)

### H2: What Counts as "Family History" for Mental Health
- **Answer-first**: Family mental-health history means more than "my parent was depressed" — degree of relation, age of onset, and recurrence all change the risk calculus.
- Cover: first-degree vs. second-degree relatives, early-onset vs. later-onset depression, recurrence pattern, why grandparent history still matters
- **Image**: Simple family-tree style graphic (can reuse/adapt existing family-history visual style from `/blog/family-history`)

### H2: Early Signs Worth Watching For in Kids With Family History
- **Answer-first**: Watching for withdrawal, irritability, sleep or appetite change, and loss of interest matters more — not less — when family history is present.
- Cover: age-appropriate signs, why irritability (not just sadness) is a common childhood presentation, when to involve a pediatrician
- **Key stat**: heritability estimates of ~30-40% for depressive symptoms from twin/adoption studies (Stanford Medicine)

### H2: How to Actually Track Family Mental-Health History
- **Answer-first**: Recording mental-health history the same structured way you'd record cardiac or cancer history — relation, age of onset, diagnosis, recurrence — turns a vague sense of "it runs in the family" into something a pediatrician can act on.
- Cover: what fields to capture (relation, condition, age at onset, treatment, recurrence), why sharing this with pediatricians/therapists at intake matters, brief mention of using a shared family health record (1 brand mention, framed as practical tool not pitch)
- **Chart**: Simple checklist/table graphic of fields to capture (or donut chart of what % of parents currently track mental-health family history vs. physical-health family history, if a supporting stat is found — otherwise use a labeled checklist table instead of a fabricated stat)

### H2: What This Doesn't Mean
- **Answer-first**: Family history changes risk, not destiny — most children with a depressed parent do not develop depression themselves.
- Cover: protective factors (secure attachment, treatment access, parental treatment/recovery), avoid fatalistic framing

### Optional FAQ Section (3-4 items)
1. Does depression run in families?: Yes — twin/adoption studies put heritability around 30-40%, and having a depressed parent or sibling roughly doubles to triples personal risk (Stanford Medicine).
2. Can eye movements predict depression in kids?: Not yet as a diagnostic tool — the Binghamton study found attention-to-sad-faces patterns track alongside symptoms in children with maternal depression history, but the researchers note the gaze shift follows symptoms rather than predicting them.
3. What age does family-history depression risk show up?: Children with a family history often show earlier onset and more recurrent episodes than those without (Columbia Mailman School of Public Health).
4. Should I tell my child's pediatrician about depression in the family?: Yes — sharing first- and second-degree relative history (relation, age of onset, recurrence) helps pediatricians assess risk the same way cardiac or cancer family history is used.

### Conclusion (100-150 words)
- Key takeaways: new study strengthens the family-history-and-brain link; 2-5x risk multiplier is well established; tracking structured family mental-health history is an underused, low-cost step
- Call to action: Start documenting mental-health history the same way you'd document any other condition, and share it at the next pediatric visit
