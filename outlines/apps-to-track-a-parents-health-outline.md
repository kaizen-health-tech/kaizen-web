### Gaps this article can fill

1. **Organized by job, with each category's limits stated.** Lists are organized by app. None seen says "no single app does it all."
2. **Consent first.** Consent shows up as a footnote. Here it opens the decision.
3. **Outdated entries.** Some results still point readers to a service that no longer exists. For example, Alexa Together was discontinued on May 21, 2025, per Amazon, and an earlier research pass found that CareZone was discontinued. Verify both before using this point.
4. **Privacy scope in plain words.** Roundups say "check privacy" without explaining that many consumer apps sit outside HIPAA.
5. **A one-page decision checklist** that works with or without any product.
6. **Non-vendor voice.** Most lists come from sellers. This one discloses Kaizen's role once and applies one set of criteria.

## Outline

### Intro and Key Takeaways (about 200 to 250 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** No single app tracks everything about a parent's health. Choose by job, and ask your parent first.
- **Key Takeaways** (house `KeyTakeaways`): pick by job; consent first; every tool has limits; check privacy and cost; a one-page checklist is at the end.
- **Statistic to find:** CARE Network and AARP, Caregiving in the US 2025: 41% of family caregivers use technology to track the recipient's health records (30% in 2020); 25% use remote monitoring (13% in 2020). Caregivers of any recipient, so word it that way. Confirm definitions in the full report.
- **Visual:** `StatTrio` with the two figures above and one more verified figure (for example, 19% use electronic task lists). Chart: none beyond the StatTrio.
- **Image:** hero (an adult child and parent looking at a phone together). Alt text describes the scene.

### H2: Before you choose an app, ask your parent (about 250 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** A tool your parent does not agree to or will not use does not help, and tracking without their knowledge can damage trust.
- **Key points:**
  - What the parent wants shared, with whom, and what stays private.
  - Tech comfort: what they already use and will keep using.
  - The conversation: offer a trial, pair it with a visit, let them stop it.
  - When capacity is in question: legal limits vary by state; point to an attorney or the care team.
- **Link:** `when-your-parent-refuses-help`.
- **Source to find:** IEEE AgeTech piece on consent and autonomy (May 2026); open before citing.
- **Image:** none.

### H2: Which job do you need done? (about 200 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Match the tool to the job: medicines, vitals, safety, family updates, records.
- **Content:** at-a-glance table: job, example tools, what it covers, what it does not cover, parent must agree to.
- **Format:** hand-written `<table>` in `overflow-x-auto`. Chart: none. Table is the visual.

### H2: Medicines: reminders and missed-dose alerts (about 280 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Reminder apps help a parent take medicines on time and can tell a family member when a dose is missed. They do not know whether a dose is right.
- **Key points:** reminder app with a family alert versus a built-in phone feature; pill dispensers as a non-app option; who receives alerts; what to do when the parent resists (link refusal article).
- **Candidate examples:** Medisafe (its "Medfriend" feature) and Apple Health Medications. Verify features, current US tier and pricing from vendor pages, with check dates. Do not repeat the Reddit claim about a subscription; the vendor terms suggest a non-US change.
- **Boundary:** no dosing, interaction or "safe" language.
- **Image:** optional screenshot only if vendor allows; otherwise none.

### H2: Vitals and wearables: what a phone or cuff can share (about 250 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Home devices and phone health data can show trends to a family member. They do not diagnose anything.
- **Key points:** Apple Health sharing (what the parent chooses to share, per Apple Support); a connected blood-pressure cuff and its app (one vendor, after reading current sharing features); the parent's device and platform decide what works; a Health Score-style signal from a wearable is a trend, not a diagnosis.
- **Candidate examples:** Apple Health sharing; Omron connect or Withings.
- **Boundary:** no accuracy claims, target ranges or advice on what to do about a reading.

### H2: Safety and check-ins: falls, location and daily contact (about 300 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Safety tools can raise an alert or show where someone is, but none of them can prevent a fall, and each has limits its maker states.
- **Key points:** fall detection on a smartwatch (Apple states the watch "cannot detect all falls," and the parent must wear it); emergency contacts; location sharing (needs the parent's agreement); scheduled check-in calls and smart speakers; cameras raise the hardest consent questions, and hidden cameras are not a safe default.
- **Candidate examples:** Apple Watch Fall Detection; Life360 or Apple Find My.
- **Boundary:** not medical alert services. Include the stated limits for each.
- **Note:** if Alexa Together is mentioned, say only that it is no longer offered (verify).
- **Image:** optional photo or illustration of a watch on an older adult's wrist.

### H2: Family updates and shared calendars (about 220 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** When family members keep asking each other for news, a shared calendar or update page can replace the group text.
- **Key points:** shared family calendar and lists for visits and tasks; an update site for health journeys; separating logistics from medical detail.
- **Candidate examples:** Cozi (free calendar and lists; Gold is paid; check current price) and CaringBridge (nonprofit update site; confirm what it does and does not hold).
- **Links:** R2 once published, guarded with `WhenPublished`; `organizing-medical-records` is for filing.

### H2: Records and sharing: portals and family records (about 250 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Records live in several places, and access rules differ: a patient portal needs the patient's permission for proxy access, even for a family member with power of attorney.
- **Key points:** patient-portal proxy access (what health-system pages say; rules vary by system and state); a shared family record you control; why a medication, allergy and diagnosis list is useful at every visit.
- **Candidate example:** MyChart proxy access, then a transition to the Kaizen section.
- **Links:** `shared-family-medical-record`, `organizing-medical-records`.
- **Boundary:** do not promise access; legal wording reviewed.

### H2: Where Kaizen fits (about 280 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Kaizen Health, which publishes this article, is a mobile app that keeps a family's health records in one place and lets people share the records they choose with the relatives helping.
- **Disclosure:** one clear sentence at the start of this section.
- **Safe to say (Product Guide, re-check before drafting):**
  - Records in one place (files, medications, notes, events), shared by choice with relatives.
  - Medication records with reminders on the phone.
  - Kai explains a lab report or answers a health question, typed or spoken, in the user's language.
  - A Health Score from Apple Health, Health Connect or Oura: a signal, not a diagnosis.
  - Group sharing: usable once the hardening release has shipped (expected about November 19; confirm).
- **Plainly state what Kaizen does not do:** fall detection, background monitoring, emergency alerts, blood-pressure readings from a cuff, importing hospital records, replacing a clinician.
- **Data handling (founder-confirmed November 17, no documents seen):** "Data is encrypted in transit and at rest." "We have signed agreements with our AI providers. Your data is not used to train their models. They do not retain it." No vendor names. No "end-to-end." No "HIPAA compliant" or "SOC 2." Confirm that each sentence matches the documented scope before it ships.
- **Callout:** house `ProductCallout`, following the Thanksgiving article's wording style. Do not add features beyond the guide.
- **Image:** optional app screenshot (confirm it shows current UI).

### H2: Privacy, consent and cost: a checklist before you trust an app (about 350 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Many consumer health apps are not covered by HIPAA, so the company's own policy is what protects the data.
- **Checklist items (the same for every app, Kaizen included):**
  - Who can see the data, and can the parent change that?
  - What does the privacy policy say about selling or sharing data? Can data be deleted?
  - Is it free, a trial or a subscription, and what happens to the data if you stop paying?
  - Does the parent know and agree?
  - Is there a plain-language explanation of how it handles health data?
- **Neutral note (legal-approved approach):** other apps exist beyond those here, this article does not cover every app, and readers should do their own research before trusting an app with a parent's medical information, including whether the company has a trustworthy privacy policy in place. No competitors named. Wording returns to legal with the draft.
- **Sources to open:** HHS OCR health-app and personal-device pages; FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (current text). Quote narrowly.
- **Visual:** checklist card (house component or table).

### H2: A one-page checklist (about 200 words)

- **Answer-first opener:** Print or save this list.
- **Content:** a short checkbox list: conversation held; job chosen; limits understood; privacy items checked; who gets alerts; backup if the tool fails; review date.
- **Format:** simple list; optional downloadable text file following the pattern of `public/downloads/*.txt` (decide; not required).

### FAQ (4 to 5 items; prune before adding schema)

1. Is there one app that tracks everything about an elderly parent's health? (No single app; pick by job.)
2. Do I need my parent's permission to track their health? (Strong recommendation; legal limits vary; link out.)
3. Are health apps covered by HIPAA? (Often not; point to the HHS page.)
4. What if my parent will not use an app? (Alternatives such as calls and a paper list; link refusal article.)
5. Can a smartwatch tell me if my parent falls? (Fall detection exists on some watches; it has stated limits.)

### Conclusion (about 100 to 130 words)

- Recap: one job at a time, parent first, check the privacy items.
- Next step: start with one tool for one job; revisit in a month.
- Related links: records guides and refusal article.
